Can We Keep Earth's Orbit Safe?

The Engineering Challenge of Space Debris

Subject: Aerospace Engineering | Topic: Orbital sustainability

Abstract

Space debris is an increasing engineering challenge because inactive satellites, discarded rocket stages and fragments from collisions occupy useful orbital regions. These objects can damage operational spacecraft and increase the cost and complexity of space missions. This article examines the main physical risks, the possibility of collision-driven debris growth, and engineering approaches that may reduce the problem. These include preventing debris during mission design, removing satellites from orbit at the end of their lives and developing technologies to remove existing objects. The evidence suggests that space sustainability requires both improved spacecraft design and coordinated international action.

1. Introduction

Satellites support services such as communications, navigation, weather forecasting and Earth observation. These systems have become important to modern society, but their continued operation depends on a usable and relatively safe orbital environment.

Space debris includes inactive spacecraft, abandoned rocket bodies and fragments produced when objects break apart. Some objects are large enough to track routinely, while smaller fragments may be difficult to detect but can still damage spacecraft. The European Space Agency (ESA, 2025) describes debris as a growing environmental challenge around Earth.

The problem is not limited to the number of objects in orbit. Their velocities, orbital paths and the difficulty of repairing spacecraft in space all affect the risk. Consequently, aerospace engineers must consider debris throughout a mission, from initial design to disposal at the end of operational life.

2. Why is space debris dangerous?

2.1 High-speed impacts

An object does not need to be particularly large to cause damage when it collides with a spacecraft. Orbital objects travel at several kilometres per second. NASA explains that debris impact speeds in low Earth orbit can average around 10 km/s, although the exact speed depends on the orbits involved (NASA, n.d.).

The kinetic energy of a moving object is described by:

E = ½mv²

In this equation, E represents kinetic energy in joules, m is mass in kilograms and v is speed in metres per second. Because speed is squared, an increase in impact velocity can produce a substantial increase in energy. This helps explain why small fragments can threaten spacecraft components.

2.2 Collision cascades

A major concern is that a collision between objects can create additional fragments. These fragments may then collide with other spacecraft or debris, potentially creating still more fragments. Kessler and Cour-Palais (1978) described the possibility of a self-generating debris population in which collisions contribute to continued growth.

This does not mean every collision automatically creates an unstoppable cascade. The outcome depends on factors including object density, orbital altitude, collision probability and the effectiveness of debris mitigation. Nevertheless, the possibility makes prevention and long-term monitoring important.

2.3 The scale of the problem

ESA's 2025 space environment report estimates that more than 1.2 million objects larger than 1 centimetre and more than 50,000 objects larger than 10 centimetres are in Earth orbit (ESA, 2025). These categories overlap: the objects larger than 10 centimetres are also part of the population larger than 1 centimetre, so the figures must not be added together.

Objects of different sizes require different monitoring and protection approaches. Larger objects are generally easier to track, while smaller fragments may be too small to monitor reliably but can still pose a serious impact hazard.

This article considers three questions: why debris is dangerous, which engineering approaches can reduce the risk, and why prevention needs to be combined with international cooperation.
