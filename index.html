Can We Keep Earth's Orbit Safe? The Engineering Challenge of Space Debris

An engineering perspective on orbital debris, collision risk and sustainable spaceflight

Abstract

Space debris is an increasing challenge for the long-term sustainability of spaceflight. Defunct satellites, spent rocket stages and fragments from collisions remain in orbit and can damage operational spacecraft. Because objects in low Earth orbit travel at several kilometres per second, even relatively small fragments can carry considerable impact energy. This article examines the scale of orbital debris, the engineering risks created by high-speed collisions, and approaches to reducing the problem. These approaches include preventing new debris, safely disposing of satellites at the end of their missions, improving collision avoidance and developing active debris removal technologies. Published data from the European Space Agency (ESA) and NASA are used to illustrate the scale of the challenge and the importance of coordinated engineering and policy decisions.

1. Introduction

Satellites support many services used in everyday life, including communications, navigation, weather forecasting and Earth observation. Spacecraft also provide important scientific information about the Earth, the Solar System and the wider universe. As more satellites are launched, however, the orbital environment becomes more crowded.

Space debris refers to human-made objects in orbit that no longer serve a useful purpose. Examples include inactive satellites, discarded rocket stages and fragments created when objects collide or break apart. Unlike objects on Earth, orbital debris cannot simply be collected using conventional waste-management methods. Removing it requires specialised spacecraft, carefully planned trajectories and reliable tracking.

The European Space Agency reports that debris continues to increase and that collisions can create additional fragments. This means the problem is not limited to the objects already present in orbit. A collision can create more debris, which can subsequently threaten other spacecraft. Long-term orbital sustainability therefore depends on preventing new debris as well as managing existing objects.

This article considers the problem from an aerospace engineering perspective. It examines the estimated debris population, the physical reasons that debris is dangerous, and the engineering measures that can help keep useful orbital regions available for future missions.

2. The Scale of the Problem

The number of objects that can be tracked is much smaller than the estimated number of smaller fragments. According to ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report, its model estimates approximately 68,450 objects larger than 10 cm, 1.5 million objects between 1 and 10 cm, and 230 million objects between 1 mm and 1 cm in the space environment covered by the report's estimates.

These categories describe different size ranges. The smaller size ranges contain many more objects, while the larger objects are generally easier for surveillance networks to detect and track. The numbers are estimates produced using space-environment modelling and should not be interpreted as a direct count of every object.

2.2 What these estimates mean

Large objects can cause severe damage if they collide with an operational spacecraft. Smaller fragments are also important because they are much more numerous and may be too small to track reliably. A spacecraft operator may therefore be unable to avoid every potentially dangerous fragment.

ESA's 2026 report also describes the increasing number of launches and payloads placed into orbit. The challenge is consequently both operational and environmental: space operators must protect current missions while ensuring that future spacecraft can continue to use important orbital regions.

3. Why Space Debris Is Dangerous

3.1 High-speed impacts and kinetic energy

Orbital debris is dangerous partly because of its velocity. NASA explains that objects in low Earth orbit travel at approximately 7–8 km/s. The average impact speed between orbital objects is around 10 km/s and can reach approximately 15 km/s.

The kinetic energy of a moving object depends on its mass and velocity. It can be calculated using the following equation:

Ek = ½mv2

In this equation, Ek is kinetic energy in joules, m is mass in kilograms, and v is velocity in metres per second. The squared velocity term means that increasing speed has a substantial effect on kinetic energy. A small fragment can therefore cause serious damage if it strikes a spacecraft at orbital speeds.

NASA's orbital-debris guidance describes how impacts can damage spacecraft surfaces, puncture components or cause a catastrophic failure. The outcome depends on the mass and velocity of the debris, the impact location and the spacecraft's design.

3.3 Collision cascades

A collision involving a satellite or another large object can produce many fragments. Those fragments can then collide with additional objects, generating more debris. This process is associated with the concept known as Kessler syndrome, described by Kessler and Cour-Palais in 1978.

The long-term concern is that certain orbital regions could become increasingly hazardous even if the number of new launches were reduced. Existing objects may continue to collide and fragment. Preventing collisions and removing selected high-risk objects are therefore important parts of protecting the orbital environment.

3.4 Engineering mitigation and compliance

Mitigation measures include designing satellites so that they can be safely removed from important orbits, reducing the chance of explosions after a mission, and planning controlled re-entry where appropriate. Operators also use tracking information to assess conjunctions and plan avoidance manoeuvres when necessary.

ESA's 2025 report described improvements in compliance with debris-mitigation guidelines. It reported that around 90% of rocket bodies in low Earth orbit were complying with the earlier 25-year re-entry standard, while around 80% were compliant with ESA's newer five-year standard. These figures refer to rocket bodies and the report's specified assessment, not all satellites or all debris.

4. Engineering Solutions

4.1 Preventing new debris

Prevention is one of the most effective ways to reduce future risk. Satellites and launch systems can be designed to minimise the likelihood of explosions or fragmentation. For example, passivation procedures reduce the stored energy left in a spacecraft after its mission by discharging batteries and removing residual propellant where possible.

4.2 End-of-life disposal

Spacecraft design should account for disposal before a mission begins. Depending on the orbit and spacecraft, disposal can involve controlled re-entry, lowering the orbit to allow atmospheric drag to remove the object over time, or moving it away from heavily used orbital regions. Disposal plans must account for spacecraft reliability, orbital conditions and the risk to people on the ground.

4.3 Collision avoidance

Tracking networks help operators identify possible close approaches between spacecraft and debris. When a conjunction presents a sufficient risk, a satellite may perform an avoidance manoeuvre. These manoeuvres require accurate orbital information, suitable propulsion and coordination between operators.

4.4 Active debris removal

Active debris removal aims to capture or otherwise remove selected objects that are already in orbit. Potential approaches include robotic capture, docking mechanisms, nets, harpoons and servicing spacecraft. Each method presents engineering challenges, including target identification, relative navigation, capture stability and the safe disposal of the removed object.

Active removal is technically challenging and can be expensive, but it may become increasingly important for reducing the risk posed by large objects in crowded orbital regions. ESA's recent reporting identifies active debris removal as necessary to address the continuing growth of the debris population.

4.5 Comparing mitigation approaches

Approach Purpose Main engineering challenge
Design for prevention Reduce the creation of new debris. Reliable design, testing and compliance.
End-of-life disposal Move inactive spacecraft away from valuable orbits. Propellant, system reliability and mission planning.
Collision avoidance Prevent collisions between tracked objects. Tracking accuracy and timely manoeuvres.
Active debris removal Remove selected objects already in orbit. Rendezvous, capture, cost and safe disposal.

5. Data and Analysis

The data presented in this article show why space debris requires several complementary responses. ESA's estimates illustrate that the number of smaller objects is much greater than the number of large objects. NASA's speed information explains why even small fragments can pose a serious threat. ESA's mitigation-compliance figures indicate that disposal practices are improving, but compliance is not universal.

The figures should be interpreted according to their definitions. An estimated population is not the same as a count of objects that can be tracked individually. Similarly, a compliance percentage for rocket bodies should not be treated as a percentage for every satellite or piece of debris. Using the correct scope is essential when drawing engineering conclusions from published data.

Taken together, the sources support a combination of prevention, better end-of-life disposal, tracking and collision avoidance, and the development of active removal methods. No single measure can remove all risk. Long-term progress depends on the reliability of spacecraft design, the decisions made by operators and cooperation between organisations using the same orbital environment.

6. What Needs to Happen Next?

Future spacecraft should be designed with disposal and debris prevention built into the mission from the beginning. This includes considering how a spacecraft will be de-orbited, how its stored energy will be removed, and how it will respond to potential collisions.

Better coordination between satellite operators can also improve the management of close approaches. Reliable tracking data, consistent reporting and clear responsibilities help operators make decisions before a collision becomes unavoidable.

Finally, active debris removal and servicing technologies require further engineering development and practical demonstration. Internationally consistent standards are also important because debris generated by one mission can affect many other operators. The objective is not simply to launch spacecraft safely, but to ensure that useful orbits remain accessible over the long term.

7. Conclusion

Space debris is a significant engineering challenge because large numbers of human-made objects remain in orbit and even small fragments can cause serious damage at high relative speeds. ESA's debris estimates, NASA's information on impact velocities and ESA's mitigation-compliance figures demonstrate the need for a coordinated approach.

Preventing new debris, disposing of inactive spacecraft, avoiding collisions and developing active removal technologies can all contribute to safer orbital operations. The long-term sustainability of spaceflight will depend on integrating these measures into the design, operation and end-of-life planning of future missions.
